This work presents a case study on the identification and validation of the nonlinear dynamic model of the multirotor mode of the DH (Hybrid Drone) prototype, a VTOL unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV). It is in this mode that vertical takeoff and landing take place, phases that are essential for autonomous operation and that demand a faithful dynamic model for control design. To obtain this model, a grey-box formulation is employed, in which the structure derives from the rigid-body equations, while the physical parameters of the aircraft, such as the moments of inertia, the thrust and reaction-torque coefficients of the rotors and the angular drag terms, are estimated from real flight data. Since the multirotor is unstable and only flies stabilized by a controller, the open-loop plant is identified from closed-loop responses, circumventing the numerical divergence and the correlation between input and states. This process follows M4V, a time-domain identification methodology. The excitation maneuvers, of the \textit{doublet} type, are applied to one axis at a time, in order to isolate the dynamics of each channel. The resulting flight data undergo synchronization, bias removal and filtering. From these data, the parameters are estimated in two phases: the equation-error method provides the initial algebraic estimate, refined by the output-error method, which minimizes the difference between the simulated response and the measurements. The identified model is validated on flight windows distinct from those used for estimation, with compound motions that excite the three axes simultaneously, showing good agreement between the simulated and the measured responses. The yaw axis proves to be the hardest to identify, owing to its low control authority and to the high inertia about that axis. Next, this model is linearized around hover and, upon the resulting linear representation, the cascade control is designed with PID controllers and their variations, tuned by pole placement. The closed loop is simulated on both the linear and the nonlinear plants, mapping the range of maneuvers over which the linearization remains representative. As its contribution, the work delivers the complete chain from raw flight data to the simulated controller and a validated model that serves as a basis for vertical takeoff autopilots, more faithful to flight data than the one obtained solely from geometric measurements and bench tests.
